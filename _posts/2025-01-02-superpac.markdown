This study investigates the impact of Super Political Action Committees (Super PACs) on minority representation in U.S. local elections following the 2010 Citizens United decision. While prior research has focused largely on federal elections, this paper uses a new dataset encompassing over 43,000 local elections across a 20-year span to evaluate how the rise of Super PACs influences the presence, entry, and success of minority candidates—defined as female and non-White candidates. Employing a difference-in-differences two-way fixed effects model, the analysis reveals that Super PACs significantly reduce the probability of minority candidates existing, entering, and winning in local races, with the most pronounced effects observed in non-partisan contests. Heterogeneous treatment analysis shows that non-White Democrats face the steepest decline in representation, while female Republicans are modestly advantaged. These findings highlight the disproportionate barriers created by outside spending and call for more nuanced assessments of campaign finance reform and its equity implications in local democracy.
